Where the Dust Remembers

Shreya Reddy

There is a kind of knowledge that lives below language — in the body's memory of a particular heat, a particular wet, the specific weight of air in a city that once held you. Shreya Reddy's debut collection, Where the Dust Remembers, moves through four decades and four cities, tracing the quiet geology of a life built across many soils. These haiku do not announce their migrations. They arrive the way sediment does: slowly, without drama, accumulating into something that only reveals its depth when you look back at the layers. Reddy works in the haiku's traditional gift for the momentary, but her moments carry the pressure of displacement. A school bag left open overnight. A stranger's name sewn into a coat cuff. Departure boards visible from a single airport seat. Each image holds, compressed inside it, the whole weight of a life in motion — the loneliness of becoming someone new, the tenderness of repetition, the strange homecoming of a lizard crossing a noon path. The form's brevity is not a constraint here but a kind of faithfulness: this is how the mind actually stores a life, in flashes, in textures, in the residue that clings after the larger story has faded. The collection moves chronologically but not linearly. Childhood seeps into transit; transit pools in the present tense of a Bangkok morning. By the final section, the reader understands that belonging is not a place arrived at but a substance accumulated — pressed flat, folded face down, carried forward. Reddy's great subject is not loss, though loss is everywhere in these pages. It is the surprising wholeness that fracture, patiently held, eventually becomes.
