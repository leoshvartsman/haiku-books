The Ledger of Salt

Riley Sato
22 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
Riley Sato spent decades moving money across continents — Lagos to London, London to Shanghai, Shanghai to Bogotá — and somewhere in that transit, learned to keep two sets of books. The official ledger was meticulous: positions held, risks managed, losses absorbed and written off. The other ledger, the one this collection represents, was kept in the body. In the posture of a six-year-old leaning over a railing. In the smell of brine and diesel that surfaces, uninvited, in the middle of a boardroom. In the shoulder that never quite unclenches. "The Ledger of Salt" moves through four cities and four weathers, tracking a life that chose discipline as its organizing principle and then had to reckon with everything that discipline concealed. These are not confessional poems, exactly — they are more like audits, conducted with the same precision the speaker turned on quarterly reports, now turned inward. What they find is not a tidy deficit but something stranger: grief that kept compounding, love that refused to be written off, a body that remembered home long after the mind had agreed to move on. The sonnets themselves participate in this tension. Their form holds, the way a banker holds — and then, in the turn of the volta, releases what the form was always secretly carrying.

Sample Sonnets

The zinc roof held the rain like an argument

that wouldn't end. We pressed our ears against

the ceiling of sound, that full agreement

between water and metal, immense

and close and democratic—all of us

equally deafened, equally awake.

I have sat in boardrooms where the fuss

of presentation filled the room. The ache

I felt was this: the sound without the rain,

the performance of weather without weather.

Someone always speaking to explain

what everyone already felt together.

Give me the zinc, the water, the shut eyes.

Give me the noise that doesn't need to be wise.

They told me fog was England's gift to thought,

that grey dissolves the edges, lets mind range.

I watched it settle on the canal and caught

nothing but cold — a cold both sharp and strange.

In Lagos, weather pressed you to the wall,

demanded sweat, demanded you be present.

Here it erased the towpath, erased all

the landmarks that might keep a stranger decent.

I stood with my A-Z and my new coat,

a secondhand wool thing that smelled of someone,

and felt the city pass without a note

of recognition — neither storm nor sun.

The fog did not teach me to think more wide.

It taught me I had nowhere left to hide.

The baijiu comes in small ceramic cups,

a courtesy the client left behind —

moutai, the good kind, and I fill and sup

alone now in the boardroom, half-resigned.

The deal closed badly. Not catastrophic —

the language of our losses is precise:

we say exposure, say systemic, traffic

in euphemism's old and courteous ice.

But here, tonight, I drink the way my father

drank palm wine after a tribunal failed,

the ritual of a man who went no farther

than the bottom of the glass and then exhaled.

Some griefs are portable. You carry them

through every city, pour them, drink them, stem.
