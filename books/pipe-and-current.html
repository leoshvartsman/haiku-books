Pipe and Current

Morgan Kim
19 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
Morgan Kim has spent a working life with hands in the ground — digging trenches, seating copper joints, reading pressure gauges in the dark beneath cities that don't know he's there. This book grew from the suspicion that plumbing and poetry share the same obsession: finding out what moves beneath the surface, and why it matters that it moves at all. The sonnets in 'Pipe and Current' follow one life across four cities — Kota Kinabalu, Manchester, the bogs and farms of Connaught, and Sydney — not as a travelogue but as a slow education in how places enter the body and stay there. Water is the book's recurring element, though it rarely behaves the same way twice. It is monsoon and frost, harbour and culvert, the pewter stillness of a February canal and the sudden release of a drain giving way at two in the morning. Kim uses the sonnet's fourteen lines the way a plumber uses a system diagram: as a form that insists on pressure, on resolution, on the relationship between what is hidden and what finally flows. These are not poems about nostalgia, exactly, though they are full of longing. They are poems about how we learn to read the places we inhabit — and how, if we are lucky, those places eventually begin to read us back.

Sample Sonnets

What I wanted: to be changed by argument,

by the hard knock of an idea against

what I thought I knew. To have it meant

something, all that distance—the dispensed

years of pipe and wrench in Irish rain,

the squelch of field-work, culverts, rural jobs,

the long drive home through dark. I came to train

myself to think differently, to probe

the thing beneath the surface. Here, at midnight,

the essay at its thirtieth draft, I ask

if change is just frostbite of a different kind—

the slow, ungrafted feeling, the dim light

of a mind gone numb behind its task,

blood somewhere it can't signal, can't remind.

The copper joint won't seat until it's clean,

the flux applied in thin strokes, left to dry,

the torch brought close but not enough to mean

the solder runs before the joint's aligned.

I learned this in a shed off the main road

from Castlebar, a man who said one thing:

*you hear it when it's right*. A kind of code

I spent three winters learning to unstring.

The boiler room is low and smells of oil,

the lagging wrapped in tape that's going brown,

a window giving onto empty soil

where the fog has settled, thick, without a sound.

I hear it when it's right. I heard it once.

The copper cooled. I didn't move for months.

The gauge trembles at the edge of what it knows,

a needle's red confession — too much, too fast.

I watch it like a man who almost slows

before the junction fails, before the blast

of pressure finds the joint I nearly missed.

Six years of this: the body learns to read

a system's mood before the first has hissed

its warning. Not instinct — something between need

and method, call it fluency in steel.

Jakarta taught me pipes that wept through walls,

that city's guts exposed at every heel.

Now Sydney hums beneath its shopping malls

and I crouch in the dark below the street,

translating what the infrastructure needs to eat.
