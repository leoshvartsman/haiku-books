The Ledger of Borrowed Light

Robin Novak
25 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
A ledger is a place where things are recorded so they won't be lost — debts and credits, what went out and what came in. But some kinds of accounting resist the column. How do you enter the smell of eucalyptus burning on a Quito morning, or the weight of a stranger's hands counting worn rupees at a Kolkata window, or the particular mercy of a supermarket's cold air on a Miami afternoon? Robin Novak has spent a working life moving between numbers and places, and this collection is the book that work couldn't contain. The sonnets in The Ledger of Borrowed Light follow a life across three cities and five decades, but they are not a memoir in any tidy sense. They are more like a running account — the kind you keep not for an auditor but for yourself, in the margins, in the middle of the night, in whatever language surfaces first. Novak writes in English about a life that was also lived in Spanish and Hindi, and the friction of that translation is part of the music here. What we carry from the places that made us, what we owe the people who labored beside us, what it means to arrive somewhere and slowly, incompletely, call it home — these are the questions the book turns over, without pretending to settle them.

Sample Sonnets

What the altitude kept it did not keep

for me—it kept for itself, the way a place

retains what passes through it, holds in sleep

the outlines of a child's upturned face.

Pichincha does not miss me. The naranjilla

vendor has long moved or aged or gone.

The eucalyptus burns without the filler

of my nostalgia, carries on, carries on.

I am Robin Novak, fifty-five, in heat

that has no memory of me, in a city

built on water, built for the defeat

of winter, and I do not ask for pity.

I ask for what the thin air always gave:

the thing seen clean, the word that does not cave.

The Cumberland at dusk goes red, then brown,

then something past brown that has no clean name—

sandstone absorbing what the day set down,

the light agreeing finally to tame.

I'd never seen a bluff that wasn't green,

hadn't known how rock could hold a color

the way Quito holds its cold between

the hours of two and four, a throat gone duller.

I wrote it wrong the first time. Wrote *like blood*,

which was the easy reach, the door left wide.

A workshop voice said: *stay with what you've got*.

I went back to the bluff. I stayed. The mud

along the trail held bootprints, mine inside

another's, each one pressed and unforgot.

The spreadsheet open, and beyond the glass

the maidan breathing in its afternoon,

a boy in white chasing a ball across

the field as if the grass were something strewn

for him alone, as if the office towers

that ring the green were scenery, not walls.

I spent the best of my accountant hours

dividing what he has no name for: calls

on capital, on time, on human cost.

The window held us both in its one frame —

the boy, the ball, the field, the boy almost

too small to find again. I saved my name

in the file header. He ran out of light.

The maidan swallowed both of us by night.
