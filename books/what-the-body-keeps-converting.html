What the Body Keeps Converting

Robin Harris

Robin Harris spent four decades solving problems that had right answers. As an engineer, he learned to trust what could be measured — load and stress, voltage and resistance, the clean satisfaction of a system that held. This book is what happened when the systems stopped, when the scheduled world gave way to open mornings and a window left ajar, when a body that had carried itself through Kolkata, New York, and Hong Kong was finally asked to simply stand still and report what it found. What the Body Keeps Converting is the record of that reckoning: patient, wry, and quietly astonished by what survives. Haiku suited Harris the way a vernier caliper suits a careful hand — it is a form that insists on precision while remaining honest about what precision cannot hold. These poems do not explain the past so much as catch it mid-arrival: diesel and jasmine ambushing a body on a Hong Kong street, fingers typing phantom messages on an MTR handrail, a rotary phone number still turning in the mouth forty years after the last dial tone. The mathematics here is not the mathematics of proof. It is the mathematics of conversion — of asking what one thing becomes when the context changes, what unit survives the translation, what remainder cannot be accounted for. The book moves through eight sections that together trace an arc from disorientation to something that is not quite peace but is close to it — a willingness to let the banyan leaf dry on the step without moving it, to let the sparrow be a sparrow without asking it to mean more than it does. Readers need not have lived Harris's particular cities or carried his particular languages to recognize the central question these poems circle: after a life spent converting energy into output, what does the body keep for itself? The answer, it turns out, is more than enough.
