The Long Way Home

Nicola Esposito
19 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
These poems were written across two decades and four cities, though they were not always recognized as poems while they were being lived. Some began as the particular weight of a stone under a palm. Some began as fog. Some began in airport terminals at hours when the self grows thin and honest. What they share is a suspicion, grown slowly into something like conviction, that the sacred does not wait at destinations — that it lives, instead, in the act of crossing: between languages, between faiths inherited and faiths dismantled, between the person who arrived and the person who, changed and uncertain, eventually left. The collection moves through Rome, Oxford, Quito, and Tuscany — not as a travelogue, and not quite as a memoir, but as a kind of spiritual cartography. Each landscape asked something different. Each gave something back that could only be named later, from a distance, in the particular quiet that follows long displacement. The sonnet form was chosen deliberately: its fourteen lines a room small enough to hold one true thing, its volta a hinge between what was thought and what turned out to be the case. A form, like a life, that contains its own turning.

Sample Sonnets

The stone of Rome is not a metaphor.

It is a specific grey, a limestone pressed

from ancient sea-beds, cooled at a certain floor

of time, and quarried, and given a crest

of sun that turns it gold by afternoon.

I know it with my palms. I know its grain

the way I know the particular tune

of my own breath after long-haul plane,

returning—something in the body's weight

that shifts, a recalibration of the spine.

The stone receives you without sentiment or gate.

It does not care that you have changed. That's fine.

It held the Empire. It can hold you too,

diminished, returned, unsure, still passing through.

The limestone wears November like a skin,

each pore absorbing what the cloud releases,

and I would stand in Radcliffe Square to thin

myself against the damp until the crease

of who I was in Rome grew soft and strange.

The Tiber floods in January, yes,

but Oxford drizzle has a longer range,

a patience Rome would never quite possess.

I learned a different kind of reverence here,

not gold mosaic, not the incense stain,

but how a wet stone archway could appear

to hold theology inside its grain.

The spirit does not only live in light—

it pools in limestone hollows overnight.

The dialect comes back at unexpected hours,

the word my grandfather used for a certain light,

the amber-grey that fills the Alban hills in showers

of late October, just before the night

makes everything official and complete.

I cannot spell it. It was never written,

just passed in kitchens, in the summer heat,

the way a thing is given, not begotten.

In Oxford I learned other words for light,

Anglo-Saxon roots, the Latinate veneer,

and in the boardrooms learned to make it bright

on demand, a product, engineered and clear.

But the old word sits below all the acquired,

a coal not spent, not spoken, not retired.
