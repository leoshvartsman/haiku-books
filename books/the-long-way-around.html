The Long Way Around

Robin Andersson
25 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
These poems began, as many things do, in the middle of something else — a grocery list that changed languages halfway down, a recipe written in someone else's hand, a trumpet note held past the point of comfort. Robin Andersson has lived and worked across Brussels, New York, Hackney, and Dublin, and this collection is not quite about any of those places. It is about what happens in the crossings between them: what the body quietly absorbs, what the memory misfiles, what survives translation and what doesn't, and why the things that don't survive are sometimes the most worth keeping. The Long Way Around is a book of sonnets, and the choice of form matters. The sonnet has always been a structure for holding tension — between the question and the answer, the octave and the sestet, the thing felt and the thing understood. For a life lived in the space between languages, between belonging and not quite belonging, between arrival and the next departure, the form offers something useful: not resolution exactly, but a container for irresolution. These poems don't argue that rootlessness is a wound, nor that it is a gift. They ask, more carefully, what it actually is — and find in that asking a way of being present to a life that has never quite stood still.

Sample Sonnets

Her handwriting tilts across the index card,

the ink gone pale as chicory steeped too long,

each letter leaning slightly, slightly scarred

by age, or by the effort to stay strong.

I hold it at the window where the low

February light falls flat across the floor,

the waxed parquet reflecting its slow glow,

and read the Dutch I learned but never wore.

She measured nothing. *Een handje*, a fist.

The spoon you use. The heat you learn to feel.

What passes between women is like this:

a grammar without rules, a broken seal.

I've cooked it wrong for years and still it's right.

The recipe is memory. The memory, slight.

The petition lay across the trestle table,

the rain outside making the room contract,

and thirty people signed it, each one able

to add a different script to the same fact.

Cyrillic looped beside a Yoruba name,

a child's block letters next to careful Greek,

the page becoming something without frame,

a document the archive couldn't speak.

I watched the ink take longer in the damp,

each signature resisting its own drying,

and thought of Brussels, rain against a lamp,

how even there the names were always trying

to mean more than a name—my own among them,

Dutch, or French, depending on who'd sung them.

The list began in English: milk, rice, bread,

then somewhere after eggs it quietly slid—

*uien, knoflook*, as though a different head

had taken over, some unguarded kid

who shopped with his grandmother down the Dansaert,

who knew the word for leek before he knew

the English, and the French, and kept apart

the three like plates that couldn't stack up true.

I found it later, smoothed it on the table,

that fold of paper crossing its own border

halfway down, the way a childhood fable

surfaces inside a adult order.

I didn't fix it. Bought what both tongues said.

Stood in the aisle a moment, then went home.
