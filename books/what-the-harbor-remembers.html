What the Harbor Remembers

Alex Patel
20 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
These sonnets began as a way of keeping track. Three cities, three versions of a name, and the persistent question of what a self actually is when you keep removing it from its context and setting it down somewhere new. The harbor in the title is real — Auckland's Waitematā, grey-green and enormous — but it is also a way of asking what any place holds of us after we have gone, and whether the answer is anything more than the shape of an absence. The form is the argument. A sonnet is a room with a turn in it, a structure that promises pressure and then release, and that shape kept feeling true to the experience of migration — the way you carry the old life inside the new one, the way meaning arrives late, after the moment has already moved on. Jazz offered the same logic from a different angle: a melody that knows it will be broken, a constraint that makes the breaking beautiful. These poems try to live in that space between the stated thing and the thing it becomes under pressure. The book moves in five sections, roughly chronological, though time here is less a line than a chord — several moments sounding at once. Readers who know Auckland or London or Beirut may find their city estranged; readers who know none of them may find something recognizable anyway. That is the hope. Place is the occasion. What happens inside it is the subject.

Sample Sonnets

A record in a Peckham flat, after ten —

the needle finding Coltrane in the groove,

and we said nothing, seven people when

the saxophone began to prove and prove.

Jazz doesn't argue, doesn't plead its case,

it offers up the break and then the turn,

the way a phrase will leave its given space

to find out what a phrase is meant to earn.

I thought of form — the sonnet and the scale,

the twelve-bar and the fourteen-line constraint,

both built to hold the pressure without fail,

both rooms where restlessness becomes complaint.

The record ended. Someone flipped the side.

I felt the hunger that I couldn't hide.

I wrote *significant unmet need* and closed the lid.

The laptop made its small mechanical exhale.

I thought about the woman with the tins. I did

what workers do: I went home on the Northern Rail —

the train, I mean, the bus, the route that doesn't care

what you have measured or left unmeasured in a day.

The Skytower was behind me. In the evening air

something that wasn't grief and wasn't peace held sway

between my ribs, the way a jazz musician holds

a note one beat past where the melody expects —

not showing off, just listening to where it folds

back into silence, which is where the form reflects

itself most clearly: fourteen lines, a turn, an end.

The need remains. The form is what the hands can send.

The bougainvillea does not know the shrapnel.

It threads its careless magenta through each hole

the way a sentence threads a break, a channel

of want so old it's learned to make things whole.

I touch the railing where the metal puckered,

where something fast and hot rewrote the steel,

and feel the rust bloom under thumb, aructured

alphabet I read but cannot feel.

From here, the harbor makes its January

statement: grey, indifferent, and immense.

A tanker holds the light. The ordinary

resumes itself with animal good sense.

The bougainvillea does not grieve the iron.

I'm learning, slowly, what that silence buys on.
