Where the Meridians Cross

Cruz Flores
25 sonnets
Download PDF Download EPUB
Cruz Flores has spent a life crossing lines — the borders between countries, between languages, between the bench and the page. These sonnets are the record of that crossing: twenty-five poems gathered from four cities across four decades, asking what it means to carry so many places inside one body without any of them dissolving into the others. Mexico City, Virginia, Brisbane, Denver. Each place left something in the bone, and this book is the attempt to name what that something is. The sonnet, with its old argument between pressure and release, between what the form demands and what the feeling requires, turns out to be exactly the right vessel for this inquiry. Flores brings to it the habits of a judge — the patience, the precision, the willingness to sit with what cannot be resolved — and the habits of someone who learned to love poetry in a second language, in a landscape that was not yet home. What emerges is neither a memoir nor a legal brief, but something closer to a reckoning: with the places that made us, the work that shaped us, and the question of whether justice and beauty, in the end, ask the same thing of us.

Sample Sonnets

The pigeons lift at dusk above the square,

a gray eruption, feathers catching last

thin light before the lampposts claim the air,

and all the vendors fold their stalls up fast.

I watched them from my father's shoulders once,

those birds becoming smoke, becoming sky,

and thought that rising up required no stunts,

no effort—just the angle of a cry.

Now I have weighed a thousand human fates

in rooms where air-conditioning hums cold,

and still I see those wings above the gates,

that effortless dispersal, that controlled

release from stone—I learned it watching birds:

to lift the gavel lightly, choose your words.

Snow on the Rotunda steps by dawn,

the first I'd seen that wasn't mountain-far,

each flake a slow negotiation drawn

between the clouds and what the buildings are.

In Mexico City we said: the cold

is something other people keep, not us,

our winters thin as paper, barely bold

enough to stop a jacaranda's fuss.

But Charlottesville gave cold a different weight—

it pressed against the window of my room,

it made the library glow, it made me wait

for something unnamed, ripening in the gloom.

I pressed my hand flat to the frozen glass.

The print stayed warm a moment, then let pass.

I know the Brisbane River in all moods—

the pewter of a Sunday morning low,

the brown churn after rain in the uplands,

the green sheen when the jacaranda throws

its petals to the current, small and bright

as any wasted beauty, drifting south

toward the bay and the Moreton open light

and the salt that takes all rivers in its mouth.

From up the valley, silt and red volcanic soil

and sugar cane and cattle yards and grief

arrive together, all the human toil

that land absorbs and rivers bring to reef.

I read the river's color every day.

It told me what the uplands couldn't say.
