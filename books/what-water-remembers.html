What Water Remembers

Sky Patel
250 haiku
Download PDF Download EPUB
Sky Patel came to haiku the way most of us come to stillness — through its opposite. A firefighter by vocation and a poet by necessity, Patel has spent years moving between extremes: between Vienna and Santiago, between Gujarati and German and English and Spanish, between the roar of a structure fire and the particular silence that follows it. What Water Remembers is the record of an attention trained by all of that — an attention that has learned, under pressure, to notice the small and the persistent, the things that outlast the emergency. Water. Weather. The body's own quiet insistence on continuing. The form that says sign here, again, again. Haiku, for Patel, is not a retreat from the political or the visceral. It is a way of staying in contact with both without being consumed by either. These 250 poems move through seven stations — childhood wonder, displacement, physical exhaustion, bureaucratic absurdity, coastal irony, political fury, and something finally resembling peace — tracing a life that has refused to settle into any single country, any single register, any single way of being legible. The compression of the form does not simplify that life. It holds it more honestly than a longer line might, the way a cupped hand holds water: briefly, carefully, aware of what is already being lost. To read this book is to practice a particular kind of witness. Patel asks us to sit with the fluorescent waiting room and the pre-dawn refinery and the ceiling memorized at three a.m., to feel the weight of a gear hook still dripping, to follow a plastic chair downstream past a bridge where the radio hums. The drama here is not the drama of flame. It is the drama of what remains after the flame — what the water carries, what the body keeps, what the street does not yet know how to receive. It is, in the oldest sense of the word, attention as a form of survival.

Sample Haikus

the Wienerwald hush—

beech leaves soften underfoot

November holds still

painted numbers, blue,

on a wall that has no street —

I write them on my wrist

hunger after smoke

not for food exactly — more

for something with weight

eight bars of hold music

looping since the cordillera —

I know all the rests

marine layer, June—

the locals call it June Gloom—

I call it: okay

seven to two—I

count the ayes on my fingers

smoke still on my hands
