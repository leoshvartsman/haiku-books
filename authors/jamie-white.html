Selected Sonnets

The cottonwood lets go in late May heat,

its seeds a slow catastrophe of white

that settles on the hoods of cars, the feet

of shopping cart corrals, the parking light

that buzzes over nothing after ten.

I used to think it wanted to be snow,

some second-chance at winter come again,

but snow has purpose, cottonwood just goes

wherever the convenience-store exhaust

and the canyon wind decide between them.

I swept it off the windshield, never lost

a moment on what it meant — the stem,

the drift, the landing. Now I'm far from there

and miss the useless beauty of that air.

Between the calls I sat inside the car

and let the rain do what the rain does best,

which is to ask nothing of you, a spar

of water on the glass, the chest

releasing something it had held since eight.

The wipers off. The radio off too.

The parking lot of a Chili's, and the weight

of the next appointment coming due

somewhere I'd get directions to and find

and smile at and extend a hand and say

I'm Jamie, here's my card, and leave behind

a folder and a pen. But in the gray

between, the rain ran channels down the pane

and I was no one's name for a small, clean grain.

After three dry days the resurrection fern

goes silver-gray and papery and still,

a body past the point of no return,

a thing the live oak carries like a bill

it does not plan to pay. Then the rain comes

sideways off the river in the dark,

and by the morning what was dead becomes

a carpet, green and opening, each arc

of frond uncurling toward the oak's rough side

like someone slowly reading a good line.

I have been that parched. I have tried to hide

the graying in me, kept it flat, kept fine.

But wet enough, the held thing will uncurl.

The oak does not ask why. It holds the world.
